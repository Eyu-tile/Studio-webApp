Production Approach — Real Footage + AI (and why)
This ad is built on two visual textures living inside the same film, on purpose — it's not indecision, it's the strategy.
The base of the film is real footage — stock or UGC that looks shot on a phone: raw, grainy, handheld. This covers everything involving the baby and any human face: the pram alone in the snow, the baby's serene face, the historical archive, the "measured it" beat, and the hands bundling the baby up. The job of this texture is one thing only: credibility. The viewer has to believe this is real, that people actually do this — otherwise the whole argument collapses. If it looks too polished, it reads as an ad and people stop trusting it.
We use real footage for the baby specifically because a baby is the single riskiest subject for AI generation. Baby faces are where generators slip the most — off proportions, dead eyes, that "almost human" wrongness (uncanny valley) — and a baby is exactly where the viewer's eye is most trained and their protective instinct sharpest. A slightly-wrong AI baby doesn't just break credibility; it trips an alarm that kills the ad in the first three seconds. So real baby footage isn't just cheaper, it's strategically safer — it takes the biggest generation risk off the table up front.
On top of that base, we drop in three "islands of cinema" — controlled, beautiful, stable, long-lens moments generated with AI. There are only three: the moving air outside (vapor, suspended snow, a strand of hair), the dead still air inside (motionless dust in a shaft of light), and the mirror shot at the end (the adult sleeping in dead air). The contrast is deliberate: when the film turns beautiful, it's because it's saying something that matters. AI lives here — in the environments, the air, the light, the texture — where it delivers beauty and where there's no human face to give it away.
One thing this split forces us to lock: continuity. The real-footage baby and any AI fragment of the baby (like the strand of hair in the moving-air shot) have to read as the same baby — same skin tone, same hair, same hat. For that reason the hair macro is best captured as real footage too, matched to the stock baby we choose. Whichever real baby we lock becomes the master reference: cheek color, hat style, pram lining — everything aligns to it.
Short version: real footage carries the humans and the credibility; AI carries the atmosphere and the sensation. Neither could do the whole film alone — real footage can't give us the controlled sensory beats, and AI can't be trusted with a baby's face.

SECTION 1 — CONTEXT
THE CENTRAL IDEA. This is the first movement of the VSL — the argumentative core that exists before any product appears. It takes a true, uncomfortable fact (in Finland, parents leave babies napping outdoors in the cold, and have for over a century) and uses that fact as a curiosity lever that refuses to let the viewer go. With each sentence, the previous obvious explanation is killed: it's not a passing fad, it's not Nordic quirkiness, it's not the cold doing the work — it's the AIR. The block sells nothing. It arms. It plants the single idea the product will need later: still air is unnatural, moving air is the natural state of human sleep. And on the last line it turns the mirror onto the viewer — "you sleep in an experiment too" — turning a fact about Finnish babies into a personal discomfort.
THE RETENTION STRATEGY. The engine is chained knowledge-gap. It's not one curiosity; it's a chain of negations. The viewer is held because each sentence opens a question that only the next sentence answers ("why leave them in the cold?" → "why does it work?" → "if it's not the cold, then what?"). It's the strongest retention structure that exists for a VSL: you never reach a resting point where leaving makes sense. The payoff isn't relief — it's identity discomfort ("this is about me"), which serves share through tribal identification and soft polarization, and emotionally primes the sale that follows. Credibility is part of the engine: if the viewer thinks it's staged, the chain breaks. That's why the found-footage register isn't an aesthetic choice, it's a strategic one — the raw texture BUYS the suspension of disbelief the argument requires.
THE JOURNEY AND THE ENVIRONMENTS. The film passes through four worlds, in this order. First, the Finnish winter exterior — snow, low Nordic light, baby prams lined up outdoors (the raw fact, the pattern interrupt). Second, a jump in time / historical depth — the same practice seen as a thing of generations, archival texture, grandparents and parents doing the same (builds authority, "not a fad"). Third, the interior vs. exterior contrast — the central sensory beat, where the camera turns cinematic for the first time: the dead, still air inside a sealed room versus the living, moving air outside. Fourth, the mirror — the world dissolves from the baby to the adult viewer, closing on the thesis that "you were sentenced to the same experiment." Each world is more intimate and more personal than the last: it starts on a distant object (a pram in the snow) and ends on a personal accusation (you).
THE STRUCTURAL TRICK / THE VISUAL RHYMES. Three anchors stitch the block together. (1) Visible breath — the vapor of the baby's breath in the cold air appears early and returns as a rhyme in the sensory beat: moving air is literally visible, and that visibility is the proof. (2) The dust/particle in the shaft of light — inside the sealed room, particles suspended motionless in a sunbeam; it's the image of "dead air" the VO names, and it rhymes by opposition with the vapor moving outside. (3) The eye that opens — the baby waking ("baby wakes up") is echoed at the end by the cut that turns the mirror onto the adult; the same grammar of "waking / realizing." The block has NO closing loop of its own — because it cuts into the product. The last frame is deliberately an open door.
THE VISUAL LANGUAGE AND CAMERA PHILOSOPHY. Two grammars alternate on purpose. The base is found-footage / documentary: handheld, phone, grain, imperfect focus, organic sway, crooked horizon — the texture that says "this is real, someone filmed this." Over that base, islands of surgical cinema enter in exactly two or three beats where the viewer needs to FEEL and not just KNOW: the still air in the room and the moving air outside. In those beats the camera goes stable, the lens goes long, depth of field goes shallow, movement goes motorized and slow. The contrast between the two grammars is itself a tool: found-footage buys credibility, cinema delivers the sensation. When the film turns "beautiful," it's because it's saying something the raw texture couldn't say.
THE COLOR ARC. It goes from the cold blue-white of the Nordic winter (5600K–8000K, desaturated, ~#C8D4DC) in the exterior, through a desaturated sepia/archive in the historical beat (~#B8A98C, low saturation, old-film texture), back to cold in the interior/exterior contrast (the sealed room in a dead grey ~#9BA0A4, the exterior in a living blue ~#A9C6D8), and closes on a raw neutral when it turns the mirror onto the viewer — no rescue grade, almost clinical, so the accusation "this is you" lands without romanticism. The only warmth in the whole block is the pink of the baby's cheeks (~#D89B8A) — human life in the middle of the cold.
THE SOUND DESIGN. The four layers evolve from "raw real world" to "interior tension." Ambient: starts as a winter-exterior room tone (distant wind, muffled by snow, near white silence) and migrates to a suffocating, dead interior room tone in the sealed-room beat — the absence of sound is the message. Foley: rich and diegetic in the found-footage (pram wheel on compacted snow, coat fabric, the rustle of a wool sleeping bag), then reduced to almost nothing in the interior (the silence of still air). Accent SFX: minimal and motivated — a tension sub-bass that enters under "here's what's crazy," a short riser under the mirror turn. No gratuitous whoosh; the block is documentary, the sound has to feel found. Music: absent or nearly — a low, sustained, sub-perceptible drone that grows only half a dB at a time across the block and never "becomes music." Emotion comes from the VO and the silence, not from a track. The VO is the architecture; everything else goes quiet for it.
THE CHARACTER(S). Two presences. (1) The Finnish baby — bundled up in wool, cheeks pink from cold, absurdly serene, breath visible. Continuity marks: the breath vapor and the pink cheeks. It's the living proof; it never suffers, always at peace — this is essential, because the copy insists "they're not cold at all." (2) The adult viewer, who only appears implied until the last line, when the camera turns to someone sleeping in a modern sealed room — the mirror. There's no hero, no voluntary gesture; both presences are passive, they receive the argument.
CAMERA NOTATION.
* Lens in mm = lens feel (16–24mm wide, 35–50mm medium, 85–135mm long/close).
* Shot sizes: WS (wide), MS (medium), CU (close-up), ECU (extreme close-up), MCU (medium close-up).
* FG / MG / BG = foreground / midground / background layers.
* Movement: push-in, pull-out, tracking, tilt, handheld, gimbal, locked (static).
* Equipment: (hand) = raw handheld; (gimbal) = smooth motorized; (tripod/locked) = static; (phone) = found-footage texture.
* Frame rate cited ONLY when there's slow-mo (e.g. 60fps→24 conform).
* Timecodes: M:SS.s (e.g. 0:04.5).
* All shots 9:16 vertical. "Vertical thirds" = rule of thirds on the frame's vertical axis.
* ⚠ = AI-generation risk shot (motionless suspended particle, refraction, vapor, face continuity) — reserve iteration / comp.
