Brief - Spin360_CO2Bubble_HomeOffice.mp4
3 ads, one edit. Same body in all three; only the hook (first ~4 s) changes. Vox-style explainer: realistic scenes of one man in one home office, with a clean animated layer on top (the bubble, arrows, labels). Narrator voiceover. David appears real.
	

The idea: when you sit still in a closed room, you breathe your own used air back in (a "personal CO2 bubble"). Air that keeps moving breaks it up. The Spin360 keeps the whole room's air moving without pointing at you. The bubble is the ad: it lights up in the hook, breaks up and comes back in [6], breaks up for good in [7], and is gone in [8]. Inspiration, not a rulebook. Use your judgment.

	Desire
	Work in air that feels fresh and keep a clear head to the end of the day. In their words: "My brain is generally mush after 2pm", "How the hell do you stop the 1pm–4pm drowsiness?", and what they wish they had: "the fresh breeze coming in is so delightful". This is a daytime ad: the payoff is the afternoon, not the night.
	Avatar
	Men 40–55 who work from home, hours at a desk in a closed room: a spare bedroom, a basement, a "glorified closet". Door shut (kids, privacy, calls), window shut (street noise, weather). They have already tried the usual fixes: less coffee, a lighter lunch, more sleep, a box fan ("does nothing but blow around my work and make me cough"). Belief to respect: "a fan is a fan, it just blows air at you." Belief to shift: the slump is not about them, it is about the air in front of their face.
	Angle
	One reason to buy: when you sit still in a closed room, you breathe your own used air back in (a "personal CO2 bubble" right in front of your face), and the only thing that breaks it up is air that keeps moving around you. The Spin360 keeps the whole room's air moving all day without pointing at you. We sell the condition (air that never sits still), not a device.
	Mechanism
	Problem mechanism (unspoken, nobody in the market talks about it): the personal CO2 bubble, named by the researchers. Solution mechanism (new, patented): the blade pulls air in from the top and the bottom and sends a soft breeze out all around the room, so the air around you never sits still. Both are visual: the bubble lights up, breaks up, comes back, breaks up for good.
	Proof
	Facts, stated as facts, numbers only on screen: UC Berkeley measured 41 people at a desk in a well-ventilated room, every one had the bubble (200–500 ppm more CO2 at the nose than the room). Harvard: 24 office workers, 6 days, more CO2 = lower decision-test scores. Plus the real papers on screen with the lines highlighted. What we do NOT claim: that the Spin360 was in these studies, that it removes CO2 from the room, or any % of focus.
	Authority
	Stacked: borrowed (UC Berkeley, Harvard, shown as real pages), founder (David Patrick, reinvented the wheel, Shark Tank, patented blade), and David's own words ("it just feels fresh").
	Awareness
	Hook: Unaware (the 2pm slump; nobody thinks about the air). [1–5] Problem Aware (the bubble, it is not your sleep). [6] Solution Aware (air that keeps moving). [7] Product Aware (the blade). [10] offer. One sequence, no jumps.
	Positioning
	Not a fan. Not cooling, not heating. The slot: “keeps the air you breathe moving when the room is closed”. Strategic enemy: still air, and air pointed at your face. We never say we are better than anything; we show a contrast (“without pointing at you”) and let the viewer conclude.
	Emotion
	Recognition ("that is me at 2pm") → surprise ("I breathe my own air?") → relief ("it is not my sleep") → want (a fresh room, a clear head to the end of the day).

	Production brief
Format: vertical 9:16, about 1:40. Narrator VO (male, calm, like a Vox explainer, not an ad read).
The character: one man, about 45, short dark hair with a little gray, light stubble, plaid flannel shirt. One home office: a small spare bedroom, door shut, blinds shut, laptop + monitor, mug, papers. Same room, same shirt, same desk in every scene (the before/after happens in the same place). The reference images are the look to match; generate or shoot to match them.
The bubble (most important element of the edit):
1. A translucent cloud around his face. Warm amber/light orange, about 30% opacity (see the S_bubble reference). Never dark, green or "toxic": this is a 45–65 audience, not a horror ad.
2. It pulses with his breathing: grows a little when he exhales, shrinks when he inhales (a 2–3 s cycle). That is how the viewer understands it is his own air.
3. Its life in the ad: lights up in the hook → stays through [1–5], a bit denser as the clock moves → breaks up and comes back in [6] → breaks up and does not come back in [7] → absent in [8].
4. "Breaks up and comes back / breaks up and stays gone" is the whole argument in one picture. Please get this right.
Pacing: hook fast (one strong shot + the bubble lighting up); [1–5] one new element on screen every 3–5 s (a label, the bubble changing, the clock); [6] slow down for the diagram; [7] the product enters and the ring spreads; [8] one cut per sentence. Calm, no frantic cutting: this audience needs "curious, not confused".
Numbers only on screen, never in the VO. The on-screen labels are in the table.
Captions: white, a few words at a time, lower third.
Visual hooks (3 ads)
Full screen, VO starts at once. On-screen text in the first second (short, so it works with the sound off). Each hook cuts straight into [1].
