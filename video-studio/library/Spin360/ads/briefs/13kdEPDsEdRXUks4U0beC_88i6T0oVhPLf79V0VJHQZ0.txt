INSTRUCTIONS TO COPY AND PASTE INTO YOUR AI FOR GENERATION:
All AI-generated images for this ad must look like documentary photography shot on 35mm film, Kodak Portra 400. Muted Scandinavian color palette — desaturated blues, grays, muted whites for exterior Sweden scenes. Warm muted amber for interior bedroom scenes. Real textures everywhere: dirty packed snow (not fresh powder), worn wool with pills, smudged glass with condensation, skin with visible pores, redness from cold, dry lips. Natural light ONLY — no studio lighting, no fill light. Overcast Nordic winter sky for exteriors. Visible lamps for interiors. No AI glow, no plastic skin, no clean perfect edges, no HDR look. If it looks like AI, it doesn't work. Every frame must pass as a still pulled from a real BBC documentary about Scandinavian culture.
Documentary, not cinematic. The camera OBSERVES life. It does not dramatize it. No one looks at camera. Scenes feel found, not staged. The beauty comes from reality, not production.
Two color worlds: Sweden exterior = cool blue-gray, 6500K. Bedroom interior = warm amber, 2700K tungsten. The contrast between the two tells the story before the audio does.
No baby faces visible in any generated image. Only strollers, blankets, bundled shapes seen from outside or above. Non-negotiable — both for creative and Meta moderation safety.

[4] Script

In Sweden parents put their babies outside to sleep in the snow. On purpose.
Negative fifteen degrees, rows of strollers parked outside cafes with babies sound asleep inside them — no parents in sight.
And these babies sleep almost twice as long as they do indoors — researchers actually measured it. Preschools over there made outdoor naps mandatory until age three, like it's not even a suggestion, it's just what you do.
And when they figured out why? It wasn't what anyone expected. It's not the cold — babies are bundled up, they're warm. It's the air. Outside, the air never stops moving, there's always a breeze doing something. You bring that baby inside, close the windows, and the air goes dead still. Baby wakes up.
But here's what's crazy — you already know this about yourself. Every generation before yours slept with moving air, open sky, breeze on their skin. Sleeping in a sealed room with air that doesn't move? That's the experiment. And nobody's body has figured it out yet.
  

So one engineer in California got obsessed with this idea, spent four years on it, and what he came up with is honestly hard to explain until you feel it. 
It pulls air in from two directions at the same time and sends it out everywhere at once, like Saturn's rings. And because of how the blade is shaped, the air that comes out doesn't feel mechanical at all — it feels like a breeze. A real one. The kind you feel outside on a perfect night that just kind of wraps around you from every direction.
And the first time you turn it on — okay this is the part that gets people — it feels like the walls of your bedroom just aren't there anymore. Like you're lying outside somewhere and this perfect air is just moving around you and you don't even realize you're falling asleep until you wake up the next morning and you're like wait, I actually feel good. Like genuinely rested, like those Swedish babies. It honestly kind of changes what you expect from your room after that.
And it's silent — like below speed five you literally won't be able to hear it. Above five it makes this sound that's like wind through trees, that natural shhhh that just makes everything in your brain go quiet. Then you drop a scent pod in and ten seconds later your whole room smells like a forest — pine, cedar, eucalyptus. Citronella in summer and bugs just disappear. That part honestly might be my favorite.
It was made by the guy who invented the Shark Wheel — literally reinvented the wheel, went on Shark Tank, got a deal with Mark Cuban, Kevin O'Leary, and Nick Woodman. First thousand people get a hundred dollars off, and honestly once you feel it it kind of speaks for itself. Go check it out.
