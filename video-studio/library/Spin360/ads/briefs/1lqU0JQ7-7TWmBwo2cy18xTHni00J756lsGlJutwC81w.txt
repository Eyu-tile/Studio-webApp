There’s a reason people who work from home run a fan even when the AC is already on.
The room is already cool enough.
The fan still comes on.
Not because the room needs to be colder.
It just feels better when the air is moving.
But then something weird happens.
The fan gets turned down.
Moved farther from the desk.
Angled off to the side.
Or left swinging back and forth so the air never stays on one spot too long.
Which raises a pretty simple question:
If people like moving air so much, why do they keep trying to get away from the fan?
Because a regular fan doesn’t really feel like a breeze.
It gives you one stream of air from one direction.
Outside, air doesn’t work like that.
A breeze just moves through the space.
That difference stuck with an inventor named David Patrick.
He’d been experimenting with an unusual blade shape for years.
And when he finally put it on a motor, the air didn’t move the way a normal fan does.
That idea became Spin360.
Instead of sending one focused stream forward, Spin360 sends air out all the way around the room.
And its blade pushes the air instead of slicing through it like a regular fan blade, which gives the breeze a softer, wider feel.
The airflow is still there. It just isn’t concentrated into one hard stream.
But the interesting part is what happens after it’s been running for a while.
At the desk, there’s no steady blast hitting the same spot all day.
Move across the office, and the air is still moving.
Papers aren’t getting blasted around.
There’s no waiting for the fan to swing back.
It can run whisper quiet, so after a while the fan just fades into the background.
But the air doesn’t.
And the office starts to feel different.
Less like a closed room with a fan running in the corner.
More like someone cracked a window on a really good day.
One owner who keeps Spin360 in the room where they work from home described it as “less direct air in your face” with more air moving throughout the room.
And that’s really the whole idea.
The point was never to make a fan that blows harder.
It was to make the room feel better to spend the day in.
For anyone who already keeps a fan next to the desk, Spin360 is worth a look.


Spin360 — Home Office Mini-Documentary
Editing Direction / Strategic Brief
CORE IDEA
This should feel like a short History Channel / mini-documentary about a strange everyday behavior: people who work from home still turn on a fan even when the AC is already cooling the room. The story gradually reveals that they are not chasing more cold air — they are chasing the feeling of moving air. Spin360 enters as the natural answer to that discovery.
	1. What This Ad Needs to Feel Like
* Observational, curious, calm, and intelligent — not like a product demo that starts selling immediately.
* The narrator is telling a story about a behavior people recognize, not directly lecturing the viewer.
* The first half should be interesting even if the product did not exist.
* The product reveal should feel earned: the viewer should already understand why a different kind of airflow would matter.
* After the reveal, the ad should become more desirable — benefits and lived experience should grow, not turn into a technical explanation.
* Editing should support the story. Do not overwhelm the VO with fast cuts, excessive graphics, or constant B-roll.
2. Audience
Primary viewer: remote / hybrid workers who spend long stretches in a home office and already like having moving air while they work.
Important distinction: this is not an ad for someone looking for the strongest possible blast of air. The appeal is a softer, room-level breeze that is easier to live with for hours.
3. Emotional Arc
* Recognition: “I’ve seen / done that.” AC is on, but the fan still comes on.
* Curiosity: Why use a fan if the room is already cool?
* Small contradiction: People want moving air, but then turn the fan down, move it away, or make it oscillate.
* Discovery: The thing they are really chasing is not more cold — it is the feeling of a breeze.
* Intrigue / authority: David Patrick becomes part of the story because he spent years working with an unusual blade shape.
* Reveal: Spin360 appears only after the viewer understands the problem and the desired experience.
* Belief: Very short explanation: 360° changes where the air goes; the blade changes how the air feels.
* Desire: The office experience improves: no direct blast, no single sweet spot, papers stay put, quieter presence, open-window feeling.
* Proof: Real WFH owner quote.
* Soft close: Spin360 is presented as something worth looking at, not pushed with a hard sell.
4. Visual Hook Variations
Use the same spoken hook for all three versions. Only the opening visual execution changes. The goal is to test which visual contradiction stops the scroll fastest without changing the story.

Open on a believable home office. Show clear AC context first — thermostat around a normal cool setting or an AC vent running — then cut to the worker turning on a separate portable fan anyway. Do not show Spin360 yet.
	The cleanest literal version of the hook. The viewer immediately sees the contradiction: the room is already being cooled, so why is another fan coming on?
	There’s a reason people who work from home run a fan even when the AC is already on.
	HOOK B — THE FAN IS POINTED AWAY
Start already inside the strange behavior. Wide or medium shot of someone working comfortably at a desk while a regular fan is running nearby — but it is pointed toward the wall / off to the side instead of directly at them. Keep a subtle AC cue in the same environment if possible. The visual should make the viewer wonder why the fan is on if they are avoiding it.
	Creates a stronger visual puzzle before the VO explains it. It previews the central tension of the ad: people want moving air, but not a concentrated stream sitting on them all day.
	There’s a reason people who work from home run a fan even when the AC is already on.
	HOOK C — FAN MANAGEMENT IN 3 SECONDS
Use a quick, grounded sequence from the same home office: fan turns on → person immediately lowers the speed / pushes it farther back / angles it away. The AC is already running in the room. Keep the actions simple and physically believable; this should feel like an observed routine, not a comedy skit.
	Shows the contradiction as behavior rather than explaining it. The viewer sees someone turn a fan on and then instantly start trying to reduce or redirect what it does — a natural curiosity gap that leads directly into the body.

Important: This is not a rigid shot list. Use the ideas below to understand the visual logic and then choose the strongest footage available. The edit should feel cohesive and real, not like every sentence was assigned a literal stock clip.

	Desk work without direct blast; papers staying on desk; person stands, grabs coffee, moves across room, returns; normal workday continues.
	This is where the ad should become desirable. Sell what it is like to live with the product for hours.
	Quiet / background presence
	Calm office, typing, call, reading, focused work. Product remains present but visually non-dominant.
	The fan should start to 'disappear' from the story while the room experience becomes more important.
	Open-window payoff
	Subtle curtain movement / natural daylight / relaxed office atmosphere. If an actual window is shown, do not imply the Spin360 literally creates outdoor air.
	The emotional payoff: less like a closed room with a machine in it, more like a room with a natural breeze.
	Social proof
	Show the real review cleanly on screen, or integrate it as text while maintaining the home-office visual world.
	Let the quote validate the exact experience the ad has already shown. Do not introduce unrelated claims.
	Soft CTA
	Clean product shot in the same office environment. Minimal branding / CTA treatment.
	Keep the tone consistent. No sudden loud sales graphics or aggressive urgency.
	6. Product Communication Rules
* Separate the two product ideas clearly:
* 360° = where the air goes: around the room instead of one front-facing stream.
* Blade = how the air feels: softer / wider / less mechanical.
* Do not spend long on mechanism. The mechanism exists only to make the benefits believable.
* Spin360 stays stationary. Do not make it oscillate, rotate its body, or behave like a traditional oscillating fan.
* Do not use fake smoke, fog, mist, or exaggerated hurricane-style airflow.
* Do not imply Spin360 actively refrigerates or lowers room temperature on its own.
* For quietness, the intended creative idea is that the fan can fade into the background. Avoid visually implying laboratory-grade silence unless supported by the final approved claim language.
* Do not make the product look tiny, tabletop-sized, or like a generic fan.
7. Editing Style
* Mini-documentary / History Channel feel: clear, calm, visual, easy to follow.
* Pacing should be engaging but not hyperactive. Let important questions and reveals breathe.
* Use visual congruence: every shot should help the viewer understand the exact sentence being spoken.
* Do not put B-roll over every line. Let strong talking points land with simpler visuals.
* Avoid trendy tech-review graphics, excessive kinetic typography, meme editing, or overproduced AI-looking shots.
* Use AI-generated footage sparingly. Prioritize real home-office footage, real product footage, real David footage, and simple visual demonstrations.
* Music should support curiosity and gradually warm after the product reveal. It should never make the ad feel ominous or overly cinematic.
* Sound design should be subtle: switch click, room tone, light office sounds, natural breeze ambience only where appropriate.
8. What Not to Do
* Do not reveal Spin360 in the first few seconds.
* Do not turn the David section into a long founder biography.
* Do not make the ad about engineering. The viewer should remember how the room feels, not technical vocabulary.
* Do not make every regular fan look terrible or absurd. The contrast should be believable.
* Do not use aggressive 'BUY NOW' energy at the end. The CTA is intentionally soft.
* Do not add unrelated benefits such as scent, sleep, AC savings, health, productivity, or other use cases. Stay on the home-office experience.
