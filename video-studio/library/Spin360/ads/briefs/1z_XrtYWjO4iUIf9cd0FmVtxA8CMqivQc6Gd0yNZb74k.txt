The hook (0:00–0:10). One of the three below.




Norway (0:10–0:25). Parents pushing a stroller up to a café, parking it outside, adjusting the baby's blanket, and walking in. Seen through the café window: they sit down with coffee, relaxed, not looking out. People walk past the pram on the sidewalk without a glance.
The tradition (0:25–0:38). Archive: grainy old footage or photographs of Norwegian prams parked outside, decades back. Then cut to today — same scene, same street, nothing has changed.
The measurement (0:38–0:48). Plain and documentary: the baby sleeping outside, undisturbed, snow settling on the pram hood.
It's not the cold (0:48–1:00). Hands bundling the baby in wool — the sleeping bag, the hat, the layers. The viewer sees for themselves that this baby is warm. Beat. Then: "It's the air."
Moving air (1:00–1:10). The film turns beautiful for the first time. Long lens. Vapor from the baby's breath drifting. Snow suspended in the air, never settling. A single strand of hair lifting at the edge of the hood. Everything alive, everything moving.
Dead still air (1:10–1:20). Cut inside. Dust hanging motionless in a shaft of light through a sealed window. Nothing moves at all.
The mirror (1:20–1:30). An adult asleep in that same dead air. Sealed room, still dust, no movement.
The reveal (from ~1:30). The product appears for the first time. From here: the mechanism, the ring of air, the breeze, the silence, the white noise, the scent pod, Shark Tank, and the soft close.

[4] Notes
* The hook must look found, not filmed. Grainy, handheld, unpolished, awkwardly framed — like something scraped off the internet. Ugly is correct here. If it looks color-graded and cinematic, it reads as an ad.
* Continuity lock: whichever real baby we pick is the master reference — skin tone, hat, wool, pram lining. Anything generated near the baby (breath vapor, strand of hair) must match it exactly. If the hair macro can be shot real, shoot it real.
* The product does not appear before the reveal. Not in a corner, not out of focus, not for a single frame.
* Country is locked: Norway. Including the callback near the end ("like those Norwegian babies"). No footage with visible signage or language that contradicts it.
* Captions: burned in, 2–3 words at a time. The hook has to work with the sound off.

VISUAL 1 — THE ROW OF PRAMS
 Opens wide: prams lined up in the snow outside a café, snow falling. Empty street. Slow push-in on one of them — the baby asleep inside, cheeks pink, breathing calm. Back to the wide: nobody around. Nobody watching.
The bet: scale. It's not one baby — it's a whole row of them. That turns "one crazy person" into "an entire country," and that's what makes the brain lock up.
VISUAL 2 — THE PRAM ALONE
 A single pram parked on a snow-covered sidewalk. Camera locked off. Nothing happens. Snow settles on the hood. Someone walks by and doesn't look. Someone else walks by. The pram is still there. In the last few seconds, a very slow push-in until you can see the baby asleep inside.
The bet: the tension of waiting. An American viewer gets physically uncomfortable staring at that pram alone — and the discomfort is what holds the ten seconds. This is the one that stops the thumb.
VISUAL 3 — THE FACE FIRST
 Opens on a close-up of the baby's face, asleep, serene, snowflakes on the blanket. Hold it. Only then does the camera pull back and reveal: the baby is in a pram, on the street, in the snow, alone — and there's a row of other prams beside it.
The bet: the reversal. It starts tender (a sleeping baby, everyone loves that) and the pullback turns the tenderness into shock. The contrast is the hook.
