Who we're selling to: the fan sleeper — usually the husband, 35–60. He's slept with a fan his whole life; his partner hates it (the wind hitting her + the constant noise). He feels guilty, he's tried everything, and he believes it has no solution. The couple is the stage — HE is the buyer. Every scene should feel like his night, his problem, his win. 


What we're actually selling: not a better fan — the end of the fight. The first fan that works for BOTH people at the same time. He keeps his air and his sound. She stops getting hit.


Tone: Male calm documentary voice, like someone explaining an interesting phenomenon — never like an ad.
Visual style: cinematic scenes that feel like real life.


* This is NOT people talking to camera — nobody ever looks at the lens. We're observing a real couple's night, like a quiet documentary.
* Camera: still or slow. Tripod feel. Let the scenes breathe.
* Light: real bedroom light at night — a lamp, moonlight from the window, hallway light. It should feel like 11pm in a real house, not a set.
* People: look like a real couple, 35–60. Normal bodies, normal bedroom, slightly imperfect. Acting is small — a sigh, a blanket pull, eyes open in the dark. Never dramatic.
* Product & demo shots (the blade macro, the ribbon test, the smoke/airflow): these CAN be polished and beautiful — clean, sharp, like a good engineering documentary. The contrast works: real life for the story, precision for the proof.


The visual spine: ONE bedroom, ONE couple, ONE night. We open in that bedroom with the old fan, and we end in the same bedroom with the Spin360. The same gesture — a hand turning the fan on — opens and closes the movie. At the start, turning it on costs her something (she pulls the blanket, turns away). At the end, nothing happens. That "nothing" is the sale.


Three mirrored shots carry the film (same framing, before vs. after):


1. The hand on the fan switch — Scene 1 vs. Scene 13
2. Her close-up in the dark — Scene 2 (eyes open, uncomfortable) vs. Scene 11 (eyes drifting closed, calm)
3. The wide of the bed — Scene 1 (old fan) vs. Scene 10 (Spin360)


The mute test: someone watching with no sound must still understand: couple + old fan = someone suffers → new fan in the middle → both asleep. If a scene doesn't help tell that, it's not needed.

2. THE SCRIPT 
Pick ONE hook, then the body runs identical to the end.


Hook 1: Almost half the country sleeps with a fan on. Every night. Even in winter.


Hook 2: Millions of Americans can't fall asleep without a fan running. Every single night — even in winter.


Hook 3: It's the middle of January. Snow outside. Heaters running. And in millions of American bedrooms, there's a fan pointed straight at the bed.


BODY (identical for all three):


And most of those beds? Two people in them. One needs the fan to fall asleep. The other one hates everything about it — the wind hitting them all night, and that droning motor sound that never stops. And it turns out there's a real reason fan air feels that bad to some people. I'll get to it.


Because the fan person isn't being stubborn. Their body needs two things to fall asleep — the moving air on their skin, and that steady sound. Both. Play them a perfect fan recording — the sound with no air — and it doesn't work. They can tell.


And moving the fan around doesn't fix anything either. Point it at the wall, the fan person loses their air. Point it anywhere else, it lands on somebody. There's no corner of that room that works. Some couples just give up and sleep apart — one in the bed, one on the couch. Over a fan.


So the guy who invented the Shark Wheel got obsessed with this. Spent four years on it. And what he built is honestly hard to explain until you feel it.


It pulls air in from the top and the bottom at the same time, and sends it out everywhere at once — like Saturn's rings. So instead of one hard stream aimed at one person, the moving air is everywhere in the room. He's in it. She's in it. And since the thing doesn't have a front, nobody's in front of it.


Now — the fan-hater is still going to feel air. So why don't they hate this air? Because of how the blades are shaped. Normal blades chop at the air — that's the beating, pulsing thing they always hated. These are built more like barn doors: they don't chop the air, they just push it — smooth and flat. So what lands on your skin doesn't feel like fan air at all. It feels like wind coming through an open window on a perfect night. And people who hate fans usually love open windows.


And those same blades fix the sound. No chopping means no motor drone. What's left is just the pure part — this smooth, steady shhhh, like somebody gently shushing you to sleep. It's the sound every white noise app is trying to fake, and this one's real, coming from real moving air. Turn it up as loud as you need — the breeze fills in a little too, sure. But it stays soft. Spread across the whole room. Aimed at nobody.


(VO stops here. 2 seconds of the actual fan audio — clean, no music. Mandatory: this is the proof.)


So look at that bed now. The fan person keeps everything — the air, the sound, as strong as they want it. And the fan-hater? The stream's gone. The pulse is gone. The drone's gone. What's left is a soft breeze and the sound people play on purpose to fall asleep.


And here's the wild part: women who hate fans — who can't stand air blowing on them — usually like this one the most. Which makes sense. They were the ones feeling that pulse all along... and the pulse is gone.


One fan. Good for both people. At the same time. That never existed before.


Tonight, somebody turns the fan on... and for the first time in years, nothing happens. No sigh. No blanket pulled up. Nobody gets up.


That part's kind of the whole point.
