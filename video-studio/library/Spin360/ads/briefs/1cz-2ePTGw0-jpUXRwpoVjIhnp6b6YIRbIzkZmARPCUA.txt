This looks like the most peaceful place in the universe to fall asleep. But the people up there can barely sleep at all — over something you'd never guess was even there. 




So on the ISS astronauts kept waking up with headaches. Every morning. Foggy, exhausted. Sleeping pills became the second most used drug up there. They tried everything and nothing worked.
Then one sleep researcher figured it out. In zero gravity air doesn't move. So the CO2 you breathe out just sits there. Right in front of your face. You're literally sleeping in a little cloud of your own breath all night.
So NASA puts a tiny fan in every sleep pod. Just enough to move air across their face. A little breeze. And that was it. Headaches gone. Sleep got way deeper.
Now here's where it gets interesting. Back on Earth gravity takes care of the CO2 thing sure. But the air in your bedroom barely moves. Like at all. All night. And your body wasn't designed for that. We were built to sleep outside. In forests. On beaches. Places where the air is always doing something. That's when we sleep the deepest. Always have.
So someone spent four years trying to figure out how to recreate that. And what they came up with is kind of nuts. It pulls air in from above and below at the same time mixes everything together and pushes it out in every direction. Like Saturn's rings. Same temperature everywhere. Air always moving always fresh. It doesn't blast you it just moves around you.
And when you feel it — okay this is the part that gets people — it feels like the walls of your room just aren't there anymore. Like your bed is on a beach in Hawaii and there's this perfect breeze coming off the water. You close your eyes and you don't even mean to fall asleep but you just do. ══════════════════════════════════════════ CONTEXT — THE FILM FROM START TO FINISH ══════════════════════════════════════════
THE CORE IDEA This is the opening (the first ~64 seconds) of a Spin360 commercial — a fan that recreates the sensation of a natural breeze for sleeping. The opening doesn't sell the product yet; it builds the problem the product solves, using a real, bizarre fact as bait: in the most advanced place ever built for sleeping, the International Space Station, astronauts can barely sleep. The reason is invisible and faintly disgusting — without gravity, air doesn't circulate, so the CO2 you exhale just sits there, hanging in front of your face all night. NASA fixed it with a tiny fan blowing a gentle breeze. Then the film makes the turn that sells everything: the air in your bedroom, on Earth, also barely moves — you sleep in the same dead air, you just never noticed. The piece ends by pointing to where we always slept best — outside, where the air is always doing something — and hands the baton to the Hawaii vignette that follows.
THE RETENTION STRATEGY The engine is curiosity, not sensation. The opening is a four-beat mystery: an absurd problem (you can't sleep in the best bedroom in the universe) → an investigation → a stomach-knotting revelation (the cloud of your own breath) → a ridiculously simple solution (a breeze). A new piece of information lands every few seconds so the finger never scrolls. The reveal that it's about YOUR bedroom — not just astronauts — is what qualifies everyone who sleeps, not only people into space.
THE EMOTIONAL JOURNEY AND THE ENVIRONMENTS (in order)
1. THE DREAM (space, surreal) — an ordinary bed adrift over the night-side Earth. Beautiful, impossible, serene. It exists to be betrayed.
2. THE REAL POD (ISS, documentary) — the same person, now an astronaut, in a cramped, cold sleep station. The dream's promise turned into a claustrophobic can. The problem lives here.
3. THE REVELATION (still in the pod) — the CO2 cloud hanging in dead air. Peak discomfort.
4. THE CURE (still in the pod) — the fan switches on, the breeze sweeps the cloud away, she finally rests.
5. EARTH AND NATURE (your bedroom → forest → beach) — the turn toward "you," and the exit into the living air outside, handing off to Hawaii.
THE STRUCTURAL TRICK THAT STITCHES IT TOGETHER The same woman and the same floating dark hair strand carry through the dream and the reality. She sleeps serenely on a bed in space; when the serenity breaks, the image dissolves and the dream-bed becomes the real pod and she becomes an astronaut — same face, same hair, only the world around her changing. That makes the transformation read as a revelation ("it was the same person, the same problem the whole time"), not a cut. Later, that restless sleep rhymes with your restless sleep on Earth.
THE RHYME THAT CLOSES THE ARGUMENT One image repeats on purpose, twice — the logical spine of the film: something hanging still in the air where there should be movement. First, the CO2 cloud suspended in front of the astronaut's face. Then, on Earth, the dust hanging still in the moonbeam of your bedroom. The same image, the same problem: the dead air up there is the dead air down here.
THE VISUAL LANGUAGE AND CAMERA PHILOSOPHY Photorealistic throughout, captured-on-a-cinema-camera — never rendered, never glossy sci-fi. Subtle anamorphic character (oval bokeh, restrained horizontal flares that are never perfectly placed), soft halation on highlights, fine film grain (8–12%), blacks crushed slightly but never clipped, skin held on the orange-yellow line. Two camera grammars alternate and the contrast is part of the meaning: in the DREAM and CURE worlds the camera is motorized, fluid, premium (gimbal/motorized head, slow controlled moves, zero shake); in the PROBLEM world inside the pod the camera is handheld, with a living documentary micro-instability that lends credibility and tightness. Lenses follow the classic grammar compressed into seconds — wider when establishing, tighter as tension rises. Light is always motivated: every source is visible in the scene.
THE COLOR ARC (tells the film without words) Warm gold (the promise, city lights) → clinical desaturated steel-blue (the pod problem) → faintly sickly cyan (the CO2 cloud, the "wrong" point) → warm amber returning (the cure) → atmospheric Earth-blue (the bridge) → cool but domestic moonlight-blue (your dead bedroom) → forest green and beach gold (the living air). The gold of the promise, stolen by the station's coldness, is recovered as amber by the cure.
THE SOUND DESIGN (four layers, always on, mixed by moment) Ambient bed, foley, accents, music. Pad born dreamy → sours at the problem → tense and unresolved at the cloud → opens into a warm chord at the exact instant of the breeze. Silence is a weapon: ~0.5s of muteness before the cloud's peak forces an attention spike.
THE CHARACTER A woman around forty, an ordinary tired person, not a hero. Real skin, no makeup, the mild fluid-shift puffiness microgravity actually causes, tired eyes with faint dark circles. Continuity marks: a small mole below the left jaw, a faint freckle on the right cheekbone. Dark brown hair, loosely tied, with several strands escaped and floating — the visual anchor of the film. She never performs: everything in her body is a passive response to the environment. Later, a second ordinary person appears asleep in a bedroom on Earth — that's "you."
CAMERA NOTATION Lenses in mm = focal feel (no distortion unless stated). FG/MG/BG = foreground/midground/background. Moves: push-in, pull-out, dolly, gimbal/motorized head, handheld, whip-pan, rack focus. Frame rate cited only when there's slow motion. Timecodes in M:SS.s.

────────────────────────────────────────── PARAGRAPH 1 — 0:00.0 to 0:10.0 — Hook: the serenity that breaks ──────────────────────────────────────────
SHOT 1 — 0:00.5 to 0:04.0 — The impossible promise
THE IDEA — This is the pattern-interrupt. The job of the first frame is to be an image the brain cannot file in under a second: a normal bed, the most domestic object there is, floating in the most hostile place there is. It is beautiful and impossible at once, and that contradiction is the hook. We open on serenity deliberately, even though the film is about the failure to sleep, because we are going to betray this peace in three seconds — and the betrayal only lands if the peace is genuinely, achingly believable first. It must read as a real photograph of a real moment, never as a render, because the whole concept rests on the viewer half-believing it could be real. It is about the BEDROOM, not the astronaut — which is what makes everyone who sleeps lean in, not only people who care about space.
THE SHOT — The film holds on absolute black from 0:00.0 to 0:00.5 — no image, no sound, the silence-before that makes the first note hit harder. At 0:00.5, on a single frame, the image ignites together with a chest-felt sub-bass impact (the first sound, co-equal with the first frame). The environment: the black void of low Earth orbit, no spaceship, no walls, no glass, no logic holding the bed up — and below, filling the lower half of the frame, the curved night-side Earth, golden city lights glittering through cloud, a soft scatter of stars behind. An epic wide, 9:16, 35mm feel (natural, no distortion, captured look), medium aperture so bed and Earth both stay legible. Camera floats as if it too were weightless, placed slightly above the bed in a soft high angle tilting down so Earth owns the base. Three layers of depth — FG: a corner of white duvet and a pillow drifting weightlessly toward the top of frame, defocused; MG: the whole bed, in critical focus; BG: the Earth. The person: a woman around forty asleep on her side, knees slightly drawn up, one hand near her face, utterly serene; dark hair with several strands floating in zero-g (the anchor strand among them); real skin with pores and the mild microgravity puffiness; the small mole below her left jaw catching a touch of light. Light is motivated entirely from the cities' golden glow far below — warm amber ~3000K licking her face and the sheets from beneath, the far side of her body sinking to near-black blue; high contrast, soft halation on the rim. From 0:00.5 to 0:04.0 the camera performs a slow orbital drift around the bed (gimbal/motorized, glassy-smooth) while the bed itself rotates ~3° on its axis; the duvet corner keeps drifting. Sound: a low dreamy music pad is born out of the sub-bass; foley of her slow, deep, peaceful breathing, mixed ultra-close. VO: "This looks like the most peaceful place in the universe to fall asleep." Transition: continuous — no cut into Shot 2.
SHOT 2 — 0:04.0 to 0:06.5 — The crack
THE IDEA — This is the turn that converts a pretty image into a story. We promised peace; now we take it away on the same body, in the same place, so the viewer feels the loss before they understand the cause. The discomfort must be involuntary and tiny — not acting, not a performance of distress, but the body failing to rest. The smaller and more real the unease, the more it unsettles. This is the "wait, what's wrong?" beat that buys us the next ten seconds.
THE SHOT — The orbital drift converts into a slow continuous push-in (still drifting faintly), closing from the wide toward a tighter framing, a 50mm feel, the Earth sliding lower in frame as we move in on her. The environment is unchanged — the point is that nothing external changes, only her. The person: she shifts, turns a little more onto her side, and her brow furrows in an involuntary discomfort; the floating strands move with the turn; her breathing, until now slow and even, catches slightly. Light holds the warm golden key from below. Sound: the dreamy pad sours and detunes a hair, and a cold station hum begins to bleed in underneath it — the first sonic hint that this paradise is actually a machine. VO, with a comma of tension: "But the people up there can barely sleep at all —". Transition: continuous push-in into Shot 3.
SHOT 3 — 0:06.5 to 0:10.0 — The dissolve (dream becomes real)
THE IDEA — This is the reveal, and it is the cleverest mechanical beat in the opening. Instead of cutting from "dream" to "reality," we transform one into the other on the same face, so the audience experiences the realization as a dawning rather than an edit: the gorgeous floating bed was never real — it was how this exhausted astronaut wishes it felt. Keeping the face and the hair strand identical across the dissolve is the entire trick; it says "same person, same problem, the whole time." The light doing the cooling (warm → clinical) is the emotion of the reveal made visible: comfort draining into a cold machine.
THE SHOT — The push-in lands and nearly locks on a tight close-up of the face, 85mm feel, shallow depth, only the eyes and cheekbone in critical focus, the rest dissolving into bokeh. From ~0:06.5 to ~0:10.0 the transformation plays as a slow ~3.5s dissolve, executed in the edit as a cross-dissolve between two identical states — dream-face and astronaut-face — NEVER a native generator morph (which turns to mush). The environment transforms around the locked face: the warm 3000K cools to a clinical blue-white ~6000K; the soft edge of the linen sheet hardens into the padded collar of a sleeping bag; the open void of space behind her closes into a quilted cabin wall. The person stays identical — same features, same anchor strand — only her context becomes a cramped ISS sleep station. Sound: the cold hum now dominates the mix, the dreamy pad gone neutral and chilly. VO, over the dissolve tail: "over something you'd never guess was even there." Transition: the dissolve settles us fully inside the pod; hard cut on the new world into Paragraph 2.
────────────────────────────────────────── PARAGRAPH 2 — 0:10.0 to 0:20.0 — The problem, tightened ──────────────────────────────────────────
SHOT 4 — 0:10.0 to 0:13.5 — The scale (it's everyone)
THE IDEA — Before we go intimate, we go wide once, to plant a fact without narrating it: this isn't one unlucky person, it's the whole crew, every night. The VO says "astronauts" in the plural and the image must pay that off — a row of identical sleep pods, other bodies also failing to rest. It also resets the eye after the close, intimate dissolve, and it lets the claustrophobia of the real environment land: after the infinite open space of the dream, we slam into a space the size of a closet. The contrast is the point — paradise was a lie, this is the truth, and it's tiny and cold.
THE SHOT — CUT, and the camera changes grammar entirely: now handheld, with a living documentary micro-instability (alive, not shaky) — the visual signature of "this is real." The environment: a cramped, lived-in ISS crew module — quilted padded walls, cable runs, velcro patches, a stowed laptop, a small personal photo taped up, general orbital clutter; a row of crew sleep pods lined up like lockers. Wide lens, 24–28mm feel, closed aperture (f/5.6–8) for deep focus so the cabin in the FG and the row of pods in the BG are both sharp. Camera at chest height. From 0:10.0 to 0:13.5 a slight lateral handheld tracking move glides slowly past the pods; in one or two of them, other vaguely visible bodies strapped vertically into sleeping bags, also awake or restless. Light: cold hard LED, cramped shadows, desaturated steel-blue (#6E8BA8), high but deliberately un-beautiful contrast. Sound: the station hum is now oppressive, ever-present. VO: "so on the ISS astronauts kept waking up with headaches every morning." Transition: cut on the line into Shot 5.
SHOT 5 — 0:13.5 to 0:15.5 — The private toll
THE IDEA — Now we go subjective and intimate: from "it's everyone" to "it's her, right now." This is the shot that makes the abstract problem hurt — a real face doing the thing every exhausted human recognizes, pressing the temple against a headache. The idea is recognition: the viewer should feel their own tired mornings in her face. It must read as genuine depletion, not glamour — the puffiness, the glassy eyes, the heaviness are the performance, and they're passive.
THE SHOT — CUT to a subjective extreme close-up of her face, 50–85mm feel, shallow depth (only the face sharp, background blurred), handheld doing a slow tense push-in of just a few centimeters. The environment is implied, not shown — we're so close that the pod is just cold darkness around her. The person: from 0:13.5 to 0:15.5 her hand floats up into frame and presses her temple against the ache; her eyes are open but glassy and unfocused, the lids heavy; the microgravity puffiness reads clearly; a floating strand drifts across her cheek. Light: cold hard LED from above, the face nearly in silhouette with detail, steel-blue, skin protected so she reads exhausted, not dead. Sound: foley of skin/fabric on the temple touch, a heavy defeated breath. VO: "foggy exhausted." Transition: cut on the breath into Shot 6.
SHOT 6 — 0:15.5 to 0:20.0 — The evidence, floating
THE IDEA — A hyper-specific, slightly strange detail to make the problem concrete and memorable, and to deliver a real "knowledge-gap" fact (pills as the second most used drug up there). Floating pills are inherently odd and oddly beautiful — they hold the eye and they prove "zero gravity" again without a word. After two handheld shots, locking the camera here gives the viewer's eye a place to rest, and the stillness quietly begins the hollowing-out that sets up the silence of the next beat.
THE SHOT — CUT to a macro, 100mm macro feel, razor-shallow depth, camera locked-off on a tripod/fixed arm (the rhythm contrast after the handheld is intentional). The environment: the same cold pod, now reduced to a soft dark field behind the subject. FG/MG: a blister pack and two or three white sleeping pills drift loose, rotating slowly in believable micro-gravity, in critical focus; BG: the astronaut a soft blur. Around 0:17 one pill drifts very close to the lens and crosses through the focal plane, going soft. Light: cold sidelight rims the white edges; the white nearly clips but never blows. Sound: foley of the plastic blister tapping a surface as it drifts; the music score begins to thin out here, deliberately hollowing the mix to set up the coming silence. VO across the shot: "sleeping pills became the second most used drug up there. they tried everything and nothing worked." Transition: the tail sets up a J-cut — the next paragraph's tension already pulling.
────────────────────────────────────────── PARAGRAPH 3 — 0:20.0 to 0:32.0 — The discovery becomes the image (the money shot) ──────────────────────────────────────────
SHOT 7 — 0:20.0 to 0:32.0 — The cloud that grows (one continuous shot)
THE IDEA — This is the heart of the whole opening and the beat people will screenshot and share. The big idea is to make the invisible visible and to let it GROW in real time so the audience watches the problem materialize on a sleeping human face — the disgust-fascination of realizing she is breathing the same dead air back in, all night. We deliberately do NOT cut away to a researcher in a lab; the "discovery" happens AS the image reveals itself, so we never break the claustrophobic intimacy and the cloud gets a long, unbroken runway to build dread. The camera stays almost frozen on purpose: the stillness of the air is the subject, so the camera honors it by refusing to move. This is the payoff of the entire mystery — the thing "you'd never guess was even there," now hanging right in front of you.
THE SHOT — No cut to anywhere; we stay in the pod for a SINGLE CONTINUOUS ~12s shot that grows before your eyes. The environment: pure cold darkness, the pod reduced to a single shaft of cold light. A close-up of her sleeping profile, 85mm feel, razor-shallow depth, with the plane of critical focus placed exactly on the band of air just in front of her nose and mouth — so that when the vapor appears, it is born sharp while the rest of the face stays slightly soft. The camera is practically locked; across the full twelve seconds it performs only an almost-imperceptible micro-push-in (a few centimeters, clock-slow). Lighting is single-source chiaroscuro: one cold sidelight, faintly sickly cyan (#5FB6A8), carving the profile out of deep crushed black, no fill, no magic light. The person sleeps fitfully now; the floating hair strands hang dead-still in the motionless air. Beat by beat: 0:20.0–0:22.0, the air in front of her face begins, for the first time, to show something, and a riser starts to climb — VO: "then this one sleep researcher figured it out." 0:22.0–0:24.5, the breath vapor begins to form and hang — faint, thin, not rising, not dissipating — VO: "in zero gravity air doesn't move." 0:24.5–0:27.5, the vapor thickens, gaining body, always motionless, the micro-push-in bringing it a touch closer — VO: "so the CO2 you breathe out just sits there." 0:27.5–0:29.0, denser still, a minimal rack focus pulling the sharpest point onto the cloud's leading edge, the riser near its top — VO: "right in front of your face." 0:28.5–0:29.0, SILENCE AS A WEAPON: all sound cuts for ~0.5s right before the peak. 0:29.0–0:29.7, into the silence — VO: "you're literally". 0:29.7–0:32.0, the climax: the cloud is full, dense, unnatural, suspended over the sleeping face, the strands also dead-still; out of the silence returns only the lowest hum plus a reverse swell deflating the riser; the music pad sustains, tense and unresolved — VO: "sleeping in a little cloud of your own breath." Transition: hard cut on the quiet into the fan.
────────────────────────────────────────── PARAGRAPH 4 — 0:32.0 to 0:44.0 — The cure ──────────────────────────────────────────
SHOT 8 — 0:32.0 to 0:36.5 — The simple fix (the real ISS diffuser)
THE IDEA — The solution has to feel almost absurdly simple to land the film's thesis: not a drug, not a machine you'd be impressed by — just moving air aimed at the face. But "simple" must NOT mean "a cheap desk fan," which would shatter the documentary credibility of a billion-dollar station; the realism of the whole pod depends on this detail reading as genuine NASA hardware. So the device is the real thing: the crew-quarters ventilation outlet — an aerospace-grade air diffuser, compact, functional, engineering-looking, mounted on the padded wall right beside her head, exactly where NASA puts it (the system pulls cabin air through a duct, a fan drives it, and a diffuser pushes fresh air across the sleeper's breathing zone to flush the CO2 pocket). It's still humble in the sense the film needs — small, unglamorous, not a glowing sci-fi prop — but it belongs to that world. The hero sound is the soft mechanical turn-on of the airflow: it's the turning point of the whole film, the moment the problem starts to die, and we want the audience to feel that onset as relief arriving. (Note: this is the NASA station's own ventilation, NOT the Spin360 — keep it reading as aerospace equipment so the later product reveal lands as "they finally brought this feeling to Earth.")
THE SHOT — CUT to a detail of the ventilation outlet, 60mm feel, shallow depth, camera locked at first. The environment: the cramped padded pod wall near the sleeper's head, where a small aerospace air outlet sits among the cable runs and velcro — a recessed, louvered diffuser/duct outlet, machined and utilitarian, not domestic, with a tiny status indicator. IMPORTANT, to reconcile image with the VO word "fan": behind the grille, the spinning blades of a small ducted fan are visibly readable through the louvers — so the viewer sees, unmistakably, that a fan drives this; it's just a real aerospace ducted fan rather than an exposed desk-fan blade. FG: the louvered grille and the fan turning behind it in critical focus; BG: defocused, her face and the still-visible cloud. From 0:32.0 to 0:34.0 a small status light comes alive and the fan spins up inside the duct: a soft mechanical onset — the deepening of the fan/duct note, the grille faintly vibrating, the blades catching the cold light as they accelerate behind the louvers, a wisp of the nearby vapor just beginning to be tugged toward the outlet — the hero diegetic sound, dry and quiet, no Hollywood open-blade whir. The light begins its amber re-entry; the station hum gains a faint "moving air" layer. From 0:34.0 to 0:36.5 the fan reaches full speed and the airflow steadies. VO: "all night so NASA puts a tiny fan in every sleep pod." Transition: as the breeze starts, a whip-pan carries us from the outlet to her face.
SHOT 9 — 0:36.5 to 0:40.6 — The breeze sweeps it away (the payoff)
THE IDEA — This is the visceral release — the exact inverse of the money shot, and it must rhyme with it visually so the audience feels the answer to the thing that disturbed them. The whole point is simultaneity: color, sound, and image all resolve on the same frame. The cloud tearing apart, the light blooming from cold to warm, and the music finally resolving must hit together so the relief is physical, not intellectual. The speed-ramp exists to stamp the precise instant of the cure — to make the eye dwell on the moment dead air becomes living air.
THE SHOT — At 0:36.5 the directed stream from the outlet reaches her face: the first strands of hair and the t-shirt collar begin to move in a clear, aimed line of airflow (this is personalized ventilation — air pushed straight across her breathing zone), and the cloud deforms at its edges. At ~0:37.0 the camera executes a very short whip-pan from the outlet to the face (a fast whip with motion blur, a short whoosh riding it), connecting cause and effect kinetically. From 0:37.0 we settle back on the close-up of the face (85mm feel, locked, rhyming exactly with the money shot), the motion belonging entirely to the air and the hair. VO: "just enough to move air across their face." At ~0:39.2 a SPEED-RAMP drops into slow motion (captured 60–120fps, played on 24) on the precise instant the cloud is torn open by the incoming stream and swept away, fraying into wisps that vanish toward the exhaust side; in the same move the light turns from cold cyan to warm amber (#E8A24C) — the chromatic pivot of the entire piece — and the music pad RESOLVES into a warm chord on the exact frame the cloud clears. The person: her brow visibly un-knots, the tension leaving her face. 0:39.2–0:40.6, speed ramps back to normal. VO: "a little breeze and that was it." Transition: L-cut — the resolved chord bleeds across the cut into Shot 10.
SHOT 10 — 0:40.6 to 0:44.0 — Real rest
THE IDEA — One clean emotional beat: peace, earned. After all the cold, this is the visual exhale — the warmth of the opening dream returning, but now true instead of imaginary. The idea is that the audience's own body relaxes with her; we deliver a single, well-held emotion rather than rushing on. It also quietly closes the color circle opened in Shot 1: the gold that was stolen is given back.
THE SHOT — A medium shot of her face, 50mm feel, the camera back to motorized smoothness with a gentle calm push-in. The environment: the same pod, but transformed by warmth — the cold steel gone soft and amber. The person: from 0:40.6 to 0:44.0 she sleeps for real, the chest rising and falling slowly, a single strand swaying in the outlet's steady continuous stream, the puffiness eased, the face genuinely at rest. Light: soft warm amber, flattering, intimate, low contrast — a deliberate visual rest after all the hard cold light. Sound: deep, calm breathing; the gentle "moving air" layer in the hum; the warm chord sustaining. VO: "headaches gone sleep got way deeper." Transition: the push begins to become the exit move; pull-out into Paragraph 5.
────────────────────────────────────────── PARAGRAPH 5 — 0:44.0 to 0:56.0 — The bridge to Earth (the turn to "you") ──────────────────────────────────────────
SHOT 11 — 0:44.0 to 0:46.0 — Leaving the station
THE IDEA — A breath of scale and a pivot. We open the frame — the opposite of the pod's tightness — to signal the story is widening out, leaving the astronaut anecdote and heading somewhere bigger. The Earth filling the window is the literal bridge "back on Earth," and the forward momentum sets up the descent toward you.
THE SHOT — The camera reverses its energy and makes a motorized pull-out toward the cabin's hatch window, the frame opening (lens widening back to a 35mm feel). From 0:44.0 to 0:46.0 the blue curve of Earth rises into frame, hard sun carving the planet's limb; cold but aspirational atmospheric blue (#6FB7E8).
